A Land of a Different Sun

A Kenyan's Gallivant Through Uganda

Uganda has a sun of its own. Not shy, not polite. Red and heavy. Impossible to ignore. It hits you immediately and refuses to let go, pressing its warmth into your skin and hair like a sticky warning. In Kenya, the sun feels white, as though it has a schedule and expects you to have one, too.

The Ugandan sun drapes everything in a golden glow that makes even the potholes look fancy. Many times, I've found myself stopping in my tracks to gape at a glorious sunset. When the rain comes, it arrives in bursts: sudden, messy, insistently wet. And with the rain come the mosquitoes, which seem to have developed a particular taste for my blood.

Whenever I went out in the evenings without a sweater—which was often, thanks to the stifling heat—the mosquitoes descended on every exposed part of my body. I ended up covered in bumps. Honestly, some of it was my own fault. I kept forgetting to apply insect repellent, and because I'm a restless sleeper, I'd sometimes kick off my mosquito net in the middle of the night. The mosquitoes would then have a field day.

They weren't the only critters baying for my blood. I was a regular victim of various insect attacks. Once, an enormous cockroach bit me as I tried to spray it with insecticide. But the worst offenders were tiny, winged, ant-like insects that had built a nest in the bathroom. Their stings were the most blindingly painful I'd ever experienced, worse than a bee's.

It took me a few weeks to locate the nest, which was tucked neatly beneath the sink. With a mixture of determination and relief, I set about destroying it. The insects, however, were not prepared to surrender their home without a fight. They launched one final attack to protest their eviction.

Uganda also has an abundance of food—an almost unreasonable abundance. They serve an entire assortment on one plate. They call it meriona, meaning everything. Order it at a kibandaski and you're presented with generous helpings of cassava, pilau, posho (ugali), cabbage, salad greens, goat meat, sweet potatoes, and more. The plate looks less like a meal and more like a group project.

As if that weren't enough, I had wonderfully kind neighbours who insisted on ten o'clock tea, an affair that somehow included tea, cassava, roasted maize, arrowroots, and the general collapse of all portion control. I would dutifully partake, help with the dishes, and then retire home for what could only be described as a medically necessary food-coma nap that lasted well into the late afternoon.

Of all this glorious assortment, my absolute favourite was the humble Rolex: an omelette rolled in a chapati, or three. The locals called it a snack, which I found deeply misleading. In my books, it easily qualified as a full meal.

The first time I had a Rolex, I very nearly asked the vendor's hand in marriage on the spot. The flavours burst beautifully in my mouth, and for a brief, shining moment, I entered a state of pure, greasy, delicious bliss. I soon discovered that every Rolex vendor had a secret ingredient, something subtle that made each one uniquely irresistible.

It became clear, however, that I couldn't propose to them all. I settled for deep appreciation and repeated visits instead. Eventually, I even acquired a preferred Rolex handler: someone who understood my order perfectly. Eggs, spicy kachumbari, avocado, and everything made just the way I liked it.

I also discovered that Ugandans have quite the sweet tooth. Ugandan chocolate is to die for, and during my wanderings, I befriended a kindly shopkeeper who quietly tipped me off whenever a fresh batch of sweets arrived. Naturally, I indulged, perhaps a little too enthusiastically, until my enthusiasm was rewarded with a rather devastating tooth cavity.

As has probably become apparent, Ugandans are generous and friendly. They're also deeply religious. By the time I settled into my routine, I'd received seven invitations to attend church services and collected plenty of flyers, particularly from the persistent Jehovah's Witnesses.

Whenever they encountered me with my overdue-for-a-retwist, semi-freeform dreadlocks and bat tattoos, they'd take one look at me and appear to decide that saving me from eternal hellfire was now their life's mission. They were determined to get me to accept our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ into my heart.

Needless to say, they also managed to save me from getting hopelessly lost in Kampala's maze of streets. They would walk me to the main road, where I could catch a matatu home. And when the Witnesses weren't around, other people stepped in. Several strangers went out of their way to escort me to the stage between eight and nine in the evening, especially when my phone battery had died. I'm genuinely grateful for that kindness.

Of course, not every encounter was quite so pleasant. A few vendors, upon discovering that I couldn't speak a word of Luganda, would inflate their prices, sometimes leaving me paying two or three times what an item was worth. I took it in stride, though I quickly realised I needed to learn the language of money. With the help of my Baganda friends, I taught myself a few useful phrases and eventually became much more comfortable bargaining.

Luganda isn't particularly difficult to follow, especially if you're Bantu-speaking. Still, I had some cheeky guides who would laugh at my pronunciation, which made me a little self-conscious. I ended up practising the Luganda I could understand but couldn't quite bring myself to speak.

I suspect that, had I stayed a little longer, I'd have earned a black belt in Luganda speaking and comprehension. It also helped that I have a culturally ambiguous face. People couldn't always tell I wasn't from there until I hit them with simanyi Luganda when they launched into a conversation I wasn't quite prepared for.

Swahili speakers seemed less common among the civilians I encountered. Most of the Swahili I heard came from soldiers. Given the language's association with the military in some of the places I visited, I got the impression that not everyone was eager to be associated with it.

At times, I grew homesick. My tongue wasn't quite used to speaking so much English all the time. I missed the ease of familiar expressions, the rhythm of home, and the small comfort of speaking without having to think about every other word.

I also noticed how many women wore their hair in short, neat afros. They looked effortless, and the style seemed perfectly suited to the climate. That, of course, wasn't an option for me. My thick dreadlocks were not built for such humidity, and I soon found myself battling an unrelenting itchy scalp. Thankfully, someone introduced me to a herbal cream that brought instant relief.

I was equally drawn to the local style: kitenge dresses that fell gracefully to the knees, paired with structured, high-shouldered tops. There was an elegance to it, both practical and beautiful.

Then there was the kneeling.

In the communities I encountered, women were expected to kneel when greeting their husbands and elders, known as jajjas. It was a practice taught from a young age, but I hadn't fully grasped it until I witnessed it up close.

One afternoon, my neighbour's daughter, who was about ten, returned from spending the school holidays in the countryside. She was asked to greet the new auntie. Then she knelt before me.

I was completely caught off guard. I felt visibly uncomfortable, if I'm honest, while my neighbours found the whole situation endlessly amusing. They even began sending her back and forth on small errands, clearly enjoying my reaction. Eventually, I told her that kneeling wasn't necessary and encouraged her to shake my hand instead.

Another thing I came to understand was the depth of community. In Uganda, when someone says they're visiting their brother, you shouldn't take it too literally. It could be a cousin, an uncle, a brother-in-law twice removed. The sense of belonging extends well beyond the immediate family, making the whole arrangement warm and slightly confusing if you're new to it.

This expansive sense of family is often accompanied by Waragi, a potent spirit made from bananas or sugarcane. And potent it is. Two shots were more than enough to knock me out.

It would be unfair to the curious would-be visitor to leave out Kampala traffic. Once, I was stuck in traffic for four hours on what I later discovered was a thirty-minute trip. Four hours in a hot, metallic container, without music or the comforting chaos of our beloved Kenyan nganyas to calm my nerves. We were literally cooking from the inside.

Bodas are an alternative, but they come with disadvantages of their own. I discovered first-hand why people carry their own helmets everywhere after my boda hit a pothole and we went flying into the bush. Luckily, neither of us was hurt. From then on, however, I became a little more cautious before boarding a boda boda.

It helps to have a regular, trusted boda guy who knows all the nooks and crannies of Kampala's roads. I also found SafeBoda, the boda-hailing app, very useful.

At red lights, hundreds of bodas would sometimes gather, and the moment the light turned green, they'd take off all at once like a swarm of bees. For all the traffic and frustration, I appreciated the conductors. They were polite, gave me my change on time, and helped me alight. I found this a welcome contrast to some of my experiences with Kenyan matatu conductors, who could be rather unpleasant and occasionally hurl insults at passengers over the smallest inconvenience.

I keep thinking I'm done, but there's always something else to mention, like the large wads of cash I found myself carrying around and the mental gymnastics required to keep up with the exchange rate. Eventually, it became second nature.

Somewhere in the middle of it all, I made friends. Good friends. Seasoned, city-smart people who knew exactly whom to call and where to go. They came in handy more times than I can count, even introducing me to what I would describe as the best boda boda drivers in Kampala. As I've learned, having a reliable boda guy is a form of wealth in itself.

I'm grateful for those friendships. It was all love, truly, especially because everyone seemed to be living out their own little love stories. Some were madly in love, others engaged, and some were expecting their first babies. It was soft and joyful, and I was happy just to witness it.

I also acquired a favourite new word: banange! I fully intend to misuse it for the foreseeable future.

I wasn't there for long, but Uganda felt like a long-lost home away from home. For all its mosquitoes, traffic, unexpected insect attacks, and the occasional overpriced purchase, it gave me generous meals, kind strangers, good friends, and memories I'll carry with me.

I know without a doubt that I'll be back. Next time, for a little longer.
