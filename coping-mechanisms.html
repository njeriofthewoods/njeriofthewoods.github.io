Coping Mechanisms

A short story by Njeri of the Woods

You are drifting in and out of sleep when your phone erupts beside you, blasting that silly trending TikTok sound at full volume. The same cheerful tune you've heard a hundred times today. It jerks you awake, heart lurching, and for a moment, you're back in primary school, running across the field while your teacher chases you, singing the song. You wake before she catches you, your stomach knotted, your mouth dry. You lie still, blinking into the darkness, waiting for your heart to settle. It doesn't.

You haven't slept in three days.

On the first night, you took two sleeping pills. On the second, five. Last night, you swallowed the remaining ten from the blister. You lay awake through all of it, listening to the refrigerator hum and your thoughts multiply. Your ears felt hot. Your heart occasionally seemed to skip a beat, and every passing car made you jump.

You were exhausted, but your brain wouldn't shut up.

Now moonlight slips through the curtains. You need to pee, but getting up might interrupt the sleep that could arrive any second. You close your eyes and pretend. Eventually, you give up and reach for your phone.

The song plays again.

By noon, sunlight seeps through the stained sheers. You wake folded into a C, your neck stiff, your shirt damp against your back. The curtains got dirty weeks ago when you tripped over the laundry pile. You meant to wash them.

You meant to wash everything.

Yesterday, you considered changing your bedsheets. You even imagined how satisfying it would feel to sleep on fresh cotton, to smell detergent instead of smoke and sweat. You pictured yourself opening the windows, gathering the clothes, scrubbing the bathroom sink.

You got as far as looking for the clean sheets.

They were at the bottom of the wardrobe, beneath a pile of clothes you had shoved inside when someone visited last month. You pulled out one corner, saw the dust, and decided you'd need to clean the wardrobe first.

You returned to bed.

Your stomach growls. You light a cigarette.

The smoke makes you cough until your eyes water. You spit a thick, dark lump of phlegm into a tissue and toss it toward the bin. It misses. You leave it there.

You haven't bathed in eight days. The shirt you've worn for four smells of sweat beneath the oud perfume you sprayed on it before your last outing. You lift the hem, sniff, and drop it.

The cigarette isn't helping. You used to vape, but money ran out.

You search the laundry for another shirt and find a yellow carton in a pocket. Inside it is a matchbox containing half a joint.

You laugh. You've forgotten about it.

The first few puffs soften the room's edges. Your shoulders relax. You masturbate later, after watching porn for nearly an hour, and experience a brief, uncomplicated pleasure. You lie back feeling almost cheerful.

For several minutes, you believe you can fix things.

Your cat is curled against your stomach, warm beneath the duvet. She has been sleeping beside you for days, abandoning the windowsill and her usual afternoon patrol of the house. Sometimes she presses her paws against your ribs, and you mistake the movement for your own heartbeat.

You remember to feed her. Every morning, or what you think is morning. You pour kibble into her bowl and watch her eat before returning to bed.

The litter box is another matter. You meant to clean it yesterday. The day before, too. Now the smell has spread beyond the bathroom, mingling with the stale smoke and damp laundry.

You pull the duvet over your nose.

The cat begins to purr.

You could shower. Change the sheets. Wash the dishes. You could even eat something proper.

You open TikTok instead.

By the time you look up, the afternoon has disappeared.

Your WhatsApp group is busy. Someone has sent a meme about being broke. Two friends are arguing about a man nobody likes. Allan has invited you to a house party tonight. You scroll up and find his private message from two days ago.

Come through. Don't bring anything. Just yourself.

Your M-Pesa balance is zero, but the party is walking distance.

For the first time all day, you feel excited.

You stand and head for the bathroom. The mirror is streaked with toothpaste marks. Your face looks swollen, your eyes red and your acne worse. Beside the sink sits your neglected niacinamide serum. You used to take pictures of your face every morning to track your progress.

You turn away.

You pee. It stings, and the urine is dark. You haven't eaten enough to have a proper bowel movement in days. The toilet smells of ammonia. You flush and leave without washing your hands.

In the kitchen, you find the last packet of chicken noodles. The gas ran out last week, so you soak them in the tepid tap water and eat them standing beside the sink.

At seven, Allan texts that he'll come to pick you up.

You look around your room. The dishes are greasy, the laundry has spread into the living room, and something sour is growing beneath the bed. You call him immediately.

I'll pass by your place. We can walk together.

Sure, he says.

You spend twenty minutes searching for an outfit. The dress you settle on isn't clean, exactly, but it looks acceptable in the dim light. Before leaving, you check her bowl. There's enough food until morning, so you top it up anyway. She winds around your ankles, nearly tripping you, and you bend to stroke her.

Her fur smells clean. You press your face briefly into it, breathing in the warm, dusty smell of her coat.

You consider cleaning the litter box before you go.

Instead, you spray perfume on your wrists and leave.

When you arrive, Allan opens his arms for a hug.

You step aside.

What's wrong?

Nothing. I'm sweaty.

He looks at you for a moment, then lets it go.

At the party, the music is loud and the lights are warm. People laugh in the kitchen, balancing drinks on the counter. Allan produces a bottle wrapped in brown paper and smiles.

Told you not to bring anything.

You laugh. You dance. Someone compliments your perfume. For a while, you forget your bedroom, the dirty dishes, the unanswered messages. You drink more than you intended.

Then Allan takes your hand and pulls you closer.

He looks at your mouth before leaning in to kiss you.

You turn away.

What's wrong? he asks.

You want him to kiss you. But you can taste the stale sweetness in your mouth, and the dress has been pressed against your sweaty skin all evening. You imagine his expression changing when he notices.

Not here, you say, laughing as though it's a joke.

Okay.

He steps back.

You spend the next few minutes watching him talk to other people, hoping he'll return and dreading that he might.

Eventually, you escape to the bathroom.

Two girls you've never met are leaning over the marble counter. One has white powder beneath her nose. They're talking quickly, laughing at something you don't understand.

Want some? one asks.

You hesitate, then agree. You've barely slept, you're hungry, and the alcohol is making your head ache. Somewhere in the back of your mind, you know this is a bad idea.

You do it anyway.

For a moment, nothing happens. Then the light above the mirror sharpens, the music outside separates into individual beats, and your heartbeat becomes impossible to ignore.

Your mouth dries out. Your stomach twists.

You okay? one of the girls asks.

Yeah.

You push past them into a cubicle and drop to your knees.

You throw up into the toilet, your body contracting painfully, your eyes watering. You stay bent over the bowl, waiting for the nausea to pass.

Outside, someone screams with laughter.

You wipe your mouth with toilet paper and leave through the back door.
